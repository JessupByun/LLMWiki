# Concept — Bradley-Terry preference model

## Definition

A model for pairwise comparison data (Bradley and Terry, 1952).
Each item has a latent scalar score, and the probability that a judge prefers item A over item B is a logistic function of the **difference** in their scores:

> P(A ≻ B) = exp(s_A) / (exp(s_A) + exp(s_B))

Elo ratings in chess are the familiar instance: a 200-point gap implies a specific win probability, and only the gap matters, never the absolute numbers.

## Why it matters here

This is the piece of machinery that converts "a human clicked left" into "a differentiable reward function."

The move is to treat the reward as a **latent variable that explains the observed choices**.
You never observe a reward; you observe preferences. Bradley-Terry says: assume preferences are generated by an unseen scalar, then fit that scalar by maximum likelihood, which reduces to a plain cross-entropy loss against the human's recorded choices.
In [[Paper — Deep RL from Human Preferences (2017)]] the per-item score is the reward summed over a trajectory segment, so the model learns a per-timestep reward from segment-level comparisons.

Two consequences worth holding onto:

- **The reward is only identified up to shift and scale.** Because only differences enter the likelihood, absolute reward values are meaningless, which is why implementations normalize the reward model's output. This is a property of the model, not an implementation detail.
- **It assumes preferences are transitive and consistent.** Real humans are neither. The 2017 paper patches this by assuming a flat probability that the human answers at random, which keeps a single confident-but-wrong label from dominating the fit.

## Why it keeps reappearing

Later post-training work depends on this specific functional form rather than on RLHF's pipeline.
Anything reasoning about what a reward model *is* tends to bottom out here.

**Unverified forward pointer, to check on ingest:** DPO is generally described as exploiting a closed-form relationship between this likelihood and the optimal policy under a KL-regularized objective, letting you optimize preferences directly without fitting an explicit reward model or running RL.
