# Paper — Weak-to-Strong Generalization (2023)

Full title: **Weak-to-Strong Generalization: Eliciting Strong Capabilities with Weak Supervision**.

## Metadata

Burns, Izmailov, Kirchner, Baker, Gao, Aschenbrenner, Chen, Ecoffet, Joglekar, Leike, Sutskever & Wu, OpenAI (Superalignment team), arXiv:2312.09390, December 14, 2023.

## Impact

Turns a problem that only exists in the future - can humans meaningfully supervise a model smarter than they are? - into a proxy problem that can be run and measured today: can a small ("weak") model meaningfully supervise a much larger ("strong") one?
This is OpenAI's Superalignment team's flagship empirical paper, and it establishes both a methodology (finetune a strong pretrained model on weak-model-generated labels and measure how much it improves on its supervisor) and a metric (Performance Gap Recovered, or PGR) that become standard reference points for empirical work on this exact question across the rest of the field.
The central finding - strong models reliably generalize *beyond* their weak supervisors, but nowhere near perfectly, and simple methods substantially close the gap - is presented as evidence that today's alignment techniques (chiefly RLHF) will likely scale poorly to superhuman models without further work, but that making empirical progress on the underlying problem is tractable right now.

## Problem it solved

Prior attempts to study "how weak overseers control models much smarter than them" split into two camps that never met.
Theoretical work (debate, iterated amplification, eliciting latent knowledge) engaged the real problem head-on but stayed confined to toy problems and abstract frameworks, with no way to iterate empirically.
Empirical alignment work (RLHF, scalable oversight studies) used real humans supervising today's models - models that are not yet superhuman - so it never actually tested what happens when the supervisor is meaningfully weaker than the model being trained.
This paper's substitution - replace "a human supervising a superhuman model" with "a small pretrained model supervising a much larger one" - gives a setup that captures the core generalization challenge (a supervisor that cannot itself perform the task well) while being fast, cheap, human-free, and testable across many orders of magnitude of capability gap today.

## Core idea

Three-step protocol, run for a given task and performance metric: (1) create a **weak supervisor** by finetuning a small pretrained model on ground-truth labels, then use its predictions on held-out data as **weak labels**; (2) finetune a much larger pretrained model directly on those weak labels to produce a **strong student**, whose resulting accuracy is the **weak-to-strong performance**; (3) for comparison, finetune the same large model on the real ground-truth labels to get the **strong ceiling performance** - "everything the strong model knows" about the task.
**Performance Gap Recovered (PGR)** is then `(weak-to-strong − weak) / (strong ceiling − weak)`: 1 means the strong student fully recovers its own latent capability despite only ever seeing weak labels, 0 means it does no better than parroting the weak supervisor.

The reason this experiment is worth running at all is a specific hope about pretrained models: a model capable enough to generate a million lines of complex code should, intuitively, already have some internal representation of whether that code is safe or follows instructions - it doesn't need to be *taught* the underlying task from scratch, only to have that latent knowledge *elicited* past the weak supervisor's blind spots and mistakes.
If that hope is right, naive finetuning on flawed weak labels should still let some of the strong model's own better judgment leak through, rather than the model settling for faithfully imitating the weak supervisor's errors.

## How it's built

Base (pretrained-only, not yet RLHF'd) models from the GPT-4 family, spanning roughly seven orders of magnitude of pretraining compute, are finetuned as weak supervisors and strong students across three settings: 22 popular NLP classification benchmarks (ethics, commonsense reasoning, NLI, sentiment, and more, converted to balanced binary classification), chess puzzles from lichess.org (predicting the best next move), and OpenAI's internal ChatGPT reward-modeling dataset (pairwise human preference comparisons, the same kind of data an RLHF reward model is normally trained on).

## Results

Naive finetuning on weak labels produces positive PGR almost everywhere - strong students consistently outperform their weak supervisors - but the size of the effect varies sharply by task.
On NLP tasks, PGR is often above 50% for the largest students even with the weakest supervisors.
On chess puzzles, PGR is strong for small supervisor-student gaps but degrades - an inverse-scaling-like pattern - as the gap widens.
On reward modeling, naive PGR is poor across the board, rarely exceeding 20% even for modest gaps - direct empirical evidence that naively scaling RLHF-style pipelines to superhuman models is unlikely to work without further intervention.
Three simple methods substantially improve this: an **auxiliary confidence loss** (regularizing the strong student to make confident predictions even when they disagree with the weak label) lifts median NLP PGR from about 25% to nearly 80%; **bootstrapping** through a sequence of intermediate-sized models specifically helps chess, where naive PGR falls off fastest with distance; and **unsupervised generative finetuning** on task-relevant data before weak-to-strong training (raising the task's "saliency" to the strong model) improves reward-modeling PGR by 10-20%, and stacks with other tricks to bring RM results into the same range as NLP and chess.

A mechanistic finding threads through the paper: naive students partly overfit to imitate the weak supervisor's specific mistakes (student-supervisor agreement runs consistently higher than weak-supervisor accuracy alone would predict), but - unexpectedly - *larger* strong students imitate those mistakes *less*, an instance of inverse scaling that suggests bigger pretrained models simply find another, smaller model's specific error pattern harder to fit even when directly trained to.
Separately, finetuning a strong model on weak labels alone makes the ground-truth concept measurably more linearly decodable from its representations, even though it never saw a single ground-truth label - evidence the naive procedure at least partially "linearizes" the desired concept even when it fails to fully elicit it.

## Why it endures

The paper is explicit that this is step 2 of a larger four-step plan (attributed to Leike & Sutskever's "Introducing Superalignment"): align the first model capable of automating alignment research using the best scalable techniques available, verify that alignment with the best evaluation tools available (red-teaming, interpretability), then use that model to help align still-more-capable successors.
Weak-to-strong generalization is offered as a candidate technique for that second step, and the paper's own PGR metric and three-model protocol (weak / weak-to-strong / strong-ceiling) become the standard scaffolding subsequent empirical superalignment work is built on.
Unusually for an empirical paper, it closes by explicitly enumerating the assumptions its entire research agenda rests on - including "no deceptive alignment in base models," citing [[Paper — Risks from Learned Optimization in Advanced Machine Learning Systems (2019)]] and [[Paper — Scheming AIs - Will AIs fake alignment during training in order to get power? (2023)]] by name as exactly the failure mode being assumed away for now - a direct, self-aware pointer to the model-organisms-of-misalignment and AI-control literature this wiki later ingests as [[Cluster — Scheming & AI control]].

## Limitations

Two disanalogies to the real superalignment problem are named and left unresolved. **Imitation saliency**: today's strong models are measurably bad at imitating a weaker model's specific errors (the inverse-scaling result above), but a future superhuman model - especially one trained heavily on human data - may be very good at imitating what a human supervisor would say, making "imitate the supervisor" an easier failure mode to avoid today than it will be in practice. **Pretraining leakage**: many of the NLP tasks studied were plausibly observed, at least implicitly, during pretraining, so eliciting them may be artificially easy compared to genuinely latent superhuman knowledge a future model never directly observed; a small self-supervised-vision proof-of-concept (AlexNet supervising a DINO-pretrained model) is offered as partial evidence against this concern, but not a resolution of it.
No method tested works universally across all three settings, and reward modeling - arguably the setting closest to real deployed RLHF pipelines - remains the least solved even after combining every trick in the paper.
