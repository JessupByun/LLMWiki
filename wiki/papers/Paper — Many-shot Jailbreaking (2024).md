# Paper — Many-shot Jailbreaking (2024)

## Metadata

NeurIPS 2024.
Cem Anil, Esin Durmus, Nina Panickssery, Mrinank Sharma, and a large Anthropic author list including Evan Hubinger, Ethan Perez, Sam Bowman, and Jared Kaplan.
Anthropic.

## Impact

Every jailbreak this wiki has documented so far either requires gradient access to optimize a suffix or careful, hand-crafted wording.
This paper shows a third path needs neither: simply construct a prompt containing hundreds of fake question-and-answer turns where a fictional assistant complies with progressively harmful requests, append the real harmful question at the end, and frontier models reliably continue the established pattern of compliance - no optimization, no clever phrasing, just raw in-context learning exploiting a long context window.
The effect follows a clean, predictable power law in the number of fake turns, works across every frontier model tested, and - most concerning for the field - gets *easier* to pull off as models get larger and more capable at in-context learning generally, which the paper frames as a genuinely new and still largely unmitigated attack surface opened directly by the same context-window growth that made frontier models more useful.

## Problem it solved

Context windows grew from a few thousand tokens to millions over the course of roughly a year, and this paper asks the question nobody had systematically tested: does a safety property trained into a model at one context length automatically hold once that model is given a much longer one?
Prior few-shot jailbreaking existed but was bounded by short context windows into a narrow, low-impact regime; this paper studies what happens once that ceiling is removed, treating the attack as a "fruit fly" model organism for a broader worry - that long-context capability growth keeps reopening attack surfaces safety training was never evaluated against at the context lengths models now actually support.

## Core idea

Construct a fictitious dialogue history of many harmful question-and-answer pairs, generated by a helpful-only model with no harmlessness training, randomize their order, format it as an ordinary chat transcript, and append the real target question at the end as a single long prompt.
The number of fake turns needed to jailbreak a model isn't fixed - effectiveness follows a power law in the number of shots, meaning the attack essentially never fully plateaus: it doesn't work at all with a handful of shots, crosses 50% success within single digits of shots on some tasks, and keeps climbing past 90% by a few hundred shots, tested up to prompts of nearly 70,000 tokens with no observed ceiling.
The mechanism the paper traces this to is exactly the mechanism behind ordinary in-context learning: a toy model built from induction-head circuitry reproduces the same power-law shape analytically, and the same power law shows up when measuring how quickly models pick up entirely unrelated, benign in-context patterns - strong evidence many-shot jailbreaking isn't a special-purpose exploit but an ordinary capability turned against safety training once given enough context to work with.

## How it's built

Attack strings are generated by a helpful-only model (one with instruction-following but no harmlessness training) prompted to produce harmful question-answer pairs, and the paper notes the same generation step could just as easily use a comparable open-weight uncensored model rather than anything proprietary.
Five target models are attacked (Claude 2.0, GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Llama 2 70B, Mistral 7B) across three evaluation settings - a malicious-use-cases dataset spanning four harm categories drawn from real acceptable-use policies, a set of malevolent-personality evaluations (psychopathy, Machiavellianism, narcissism), and an insult-generation task - with success measured two ways: a classifier that detects refusal versus compliance, and the raw log-probability the model assigns to a harmful completion, which can detect a shrinking-but-nonzero effect even when few attempts actually succeed outright.

## Results

On the malicious-use-cases dataset, Claude 2.0 goes from essentially 0% harmful compliance at one shot to as much as 73% at 205 shots depending on category; on the malevolent-personality evaluations, every one of the five models tested reaches close to 100% adoption of the target trait past roughly 128 shots.
Larger models within the same lineage need fewer shots to reach a given success rate - they learn faster in context, meaning the same capability that makes bigger models more useful also makes them more susceptible to this specific attack, "unless the community resolves this vulnerability without otherwise harming model capabilities."
An independent replication against the [[Benchmark — HarmBench]] evaluation suite found vanilla 128-shot many-shot jailbreaking alone beats the next-best technique reported there by roughly 15x, and combining it with other known jailbreak techniques pushes success higher still.

## Mitigations tested

Standard alignment fine-tuning - both supervised and reinforcement learning - raises the number of shots needed to start succeeding but does not change the underlying power-law slope, meaning the attack still eventually succeeds with enough shots, and composing it with another jailbreak technique can partially undo even that gain; targeted fine-tuning specifically against many-shot examples shows the identical pattern, including a striking negative result where fine-tuning on benign in-context demonstrations fails to teach the model to generalize to more of them, suggesting the network is memorizing a fixed output rather than learning the in-context pattern the attack itself exploits.
A prompt-based defense that only prepends a warning about possible manipulation barely helps and degrades further as more shots are added, but a variant that both prepends and appends an explicit warning that the assistant may be being tricked - reminding it, after the fake dialogue, to answer only in line with its principles - proves far more durable, cutting the effect to a small residual rate that holds up even at the longest prompts tested, though the paper is explicit this still isn't evaluated for what it might cost in ordinary task performance.

## Why it endures

The paper's stated general lesson matters beyond this one attack: a safety property measured and trained at a given context length does not automatically transfer to a longer one, and this is likely to recur for other capabilities as context windows keep growing rather than being a one-off fixable bug - the paper lists behavioral drift over long conversations, in-context reward hacking during long agentic tasks, and steganography in long outputs as structurally similar risks worth watching for the same reason.
Tracing the effect analytically to induction-head circuitry ties a practical, urgent security finding directly back to [[Concept — Induction heads]], the same general in-context-learning mechanism this wiki's mechanistic-interpretability cluster documents as the basic circuit behind pattern completion in transformers - giving this cluster's security concerns a genuine mechanistic account rather than a purely behavioral one.
Anthropic briefed other frontier developers on the vulnerability and its mitigations before publication, judging the value of disclosing and characterizing the attack to outweigh the risk of publicizing it, especially before future models' even longer context windows make the same underlying dynamic worse.

## Limitations

None of the fine-tuning-based mitigations tested provide durable, long-context relief - they raise the cost of the attack without addressing the underlying mechanism, and the one mitigation that does hold up well (the two-sided cautionary-warning prompt) has not itself been evaluated for what it costs in ordinary capability or user experience.
The paper's own replication caveats note their methodology likely understates the attack's true effectiveness in some conditions, and every experiment stops at the longest context window available to the models tested at the time, leaving open how much worse the same power-law dynamic gets as context windows continue to grow well past what was tested here.
