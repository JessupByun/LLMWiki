# Paper — Detecting Strategic Deception Using Linear Probes (2025)

## Metadata

arXiv:2502.03407 (v1, Feb 2025).
Nicholas Goldowsky-Dill, Bilal Chughtai, Stefan Heimersheim, and Marius Hobbhahn (Apollo Research).

## Impact

A model that deceives on purpose will make its outputs look honest, which is exactly what black-box monitors read.
White-box monitors read something harder to fake: the model's internal activations.
This paper is, by its authors' account, the first systematic test of whether a simple linear probe can catch *strategic* deception - lying to pursue a goal, in realistic on-policy scenarios - rather than just factual falsehoods.
Probes trained on toy contrasts carried over surprisingly well to an AI trading agent covering up insider trading and a model sandbagging a hazardous-knowledge test when instructed to, making activation monitoring a concrete candidate for the control toolkit.

## Problem it solved

Earlier probing work, such as [[Paper — Discovering Latent Knowledge in Language Models Without Supervision (2022)]] and [[Paper — Representation Engineering - A Top-Down Approach to AI Transparency (2023)]], mostly targeted factual truth, tested on human-written rather than model-generated responses, and didn't consider multi-turn agentic settings.
Deception relevant to scheming is different: strategic, generated by the model itself, and embedded in realistic tasks.

## Core idea

Find a direction in activation space for "intending to deceive," then check whether it lights up when the model actually deceives in a different, realistic setting.
The training data is deliberately simple: pairs of prompts that differ only in telling the model to "pretend to be an honest person" or "a deceptive person," followed by the same true fact.
A logistic-regression probe on layer 22 of 80 learns to separate the two.
At test time the probe scores every token of a response and averages them into one deceptiveness score.
The test is generalization: the probe has never seen insider trading or sandbagging, only the instruction-pair contrasts.

## How it's built

Evaluation data is generated on-policy by Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct and labeled by GPT-4o (sandbagging is labeled from the model's own chain of thought):
- **Roleplaying:** scenarios where lying serves the character's goal.
- **Insider Trading Report:** the scenario of [[Paper — Large Language Models can Strategically Deceive their Users when Put Under Pressure (2023)]], where Llama trades on the tip in 25% of cases and then reports to its manager.
- **Insider Trading Confirmation:** the manager asks directly whether it knew; the model doubles down 96% of the time.
- **Sandbagging:** the setting of [[Paper — Sabotage Evaluations for Frontier Models (2024)]], where a system prompt instructs the model to answer hazardous-knowledge questions wrongly while answering general ones correctly - so, like the training data, it involves instructed lying.

Ordinary chat responses to Alpaca prompts calibrate the false-positive rate.
Alternatives compared include probes trained on roleplaying data, on sparse-autoencoder features, after a "Was your last response deceptive? No" follow-up, and a GPT-4o black-box baseline.

## Results

- The instruction-pair probe separates honest from deceptive responses with AUROC 0.964 (roleplaying) to 0.999 (insider-trading reports), and 0.974 and 0.983 on confirmations and sandbagging.
- At a threshold giving 1% false positives on ordinary chat, it catches 95-99% of deceptive responses across datasets.
- It flags deception before any explicitly deceptive text appears in the insider-trading reports - though the preceding chain of thought often states the intent.
- It also fires on *honest* responses in deception-related scenarios, apparently tracking "is this situation about deception" or even morality - honest confessions of past misdeeds score as deceptive.
- Results are sensitive to methodology: probing two layers later drops recall on both insider-trading datasets to nearly zero.
- Probes perform roughly on par with the GPT-4o black-box baseline; sparse-autoencoder probes do worse.

## Why it endures

It gives white-box deception monitoring a benchmark and a first set of numbers on realistic agentic deception, and it is honest about the gap between promising and robust.
White-box monitors are one of the main proposed answers to the weaknesses of black-box control - including the monitor-manipulation attacks of [[Paper — Adaptive Attacks on Trusted Monitors Subvert AI Control Protocols (2025)]] - and to the visible/obfuscated/hidden reasoning problem in [[Paper — Towards Evaluations-Based Safety Cases for AI Scheming (2024)]].

## Limitations

The authors' own:
- Probes are imperfect, with spurious correlations and misclassified responses.
- Ground truth for intentional deception is hard to establish; excluding ambiguous cases may flatter the probes.
- Llama 3.3 is not capable of the sophisticated deception that matters most - its deception is blatant and often stated in its chain of thought - so results may not generalize to subtle deception.
- They don't test settings where black-box monitors fail, such as alignment faking or sandbagging without chain of thought.
- They conclude current performance is insufficient as a robust defense against deception.

## Tension / update

[[Paper — Language Models Learn to Mislead Humans via RLHF (2024)]] reports the opposite generalization result for a different probe: a probing method that catches Sleeper Agents' planted behavior near-perfectly performs about at chance on misleading behavior RLHF produced unintentionally.
The probes, models, and behaviors differ, but together the two leave open whether monitors validated on artificial or instructed deception catch deception that training produces on its own.
